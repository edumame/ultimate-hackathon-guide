# Why AI Is Changing the Way We Run Hackathons

The hacker's journey has transformed. The organizer's playbook is next — and it's already happening quietly.

## Where this guide comes from

My name is Chinat Yu. I'm a hackathon researcher at Stanford, and over the past five years I've attended more than 50 hackathons across the world — first as the freshman who walked into his first hackathon barely knowing how to code, felt completely lost during team formation, and ended up just grabbing some swag and leaving. Years later I came back, teamed up with novices like my former self, and started winning. A project my team built at a collegiate hackathon is now deployed at Stanford's medical school. Hackathons changed the trajectory of my career, and I've spent my research career since trying to understand how they work as learning environments — including a Stanford-published white paper on running inclusive hackathons and a master's thesis on the hacker learning experience.

This guide is the organizer-facing half of that work: an ongoing project to interview **100 hackathon organizers** and distill what they actually do into a practical playbook, co-published with **freeCodeCamp** and Quincy Larson. Sixteen interviews in, the picture is already sharper than anything we've seen written down — organizers running everything from a first-ever 150-person college event in Canada to a 3,000-hacker flagship on the U.S. West Coast, a six-season event in India, and one of Europe's largest hackathons, which runs as an actual company with €800K in annual revenue.

## What AI is actually doing to hackathons

We have all witnessed the transformation in the hacker's journey — from vim to ChatGPT, from dev tools to terminal coding agents. But the organizer's side is where the more interesting shifts are happening, and they cut in surprising directions.

**Submissions bifurcated.** An organizer at one elite-university flagship put it precisely: the *average* submission has gotten worse — polished UIs, less effort, "too easy to create" — while the *top* of the field is more creative and competitive than ever, because skilled builders amplify themselves with AI. Hardware projects increasingly win, partly because AI hasn't conquered that space yet. Hackathons, in his words, are drifting toward "who can prompt the best" — and the differentiator is shifting from coding effort to idea quality.

**Plagiarism detection broke.** The classic red flag — a giant first commit — no longer means anything, because vibe coding legitimately produces giant first commits. Organizers at multiple events told us they allow AI-built projects but have no reliable way to catch stolen ones anymore. This is an unsolved problem across the ecosystem.

**Organizer tooling went AI-native — unevenly.** One executive director we interviewed vibe-coded her event's entire admin portal (application grading with a reviewer leaderboard, waiver batch-approval, QR check-in, per-meal scanning) and told us her quality of life transformed. A European org runs AI note-takers on every sponsor call and lets AI update the CRM automatically. A marketing lead at a 1,200-hacker event says his team uses AI "for literally everything" in content. Meanwhile another flagship uses almost no AI at all in organizing — and hand-reads 15,000 applications on principle.

**The AI-application arms race started.** Organizers now sit across a spectrum: one team auto-rejects any application that reads as GPT-written (the essay itself signals low effort, and effort is their best attendance predictor); another accepts AI-assisted applications knowingly, reasoning that a thoughtful response processed through AI still signals genuine intent; two flagships refuse AI in review entirely and treat hand-reading as a cultural ritual. There is no consensus — but every team has had to take a position.

**Old scaffolding died.** One flagship killed its GitHub starter kits this year "just because of AI" — static boilerplate can't compete with tailored AI help. The same team is repositioning mentors away from debugging (hackers ask Claude to fix their code now) toward big-picture ideas, design critique, and pitch coaching. What AI didn't kill, it's forcing to move up the stack.

## What the fundamentals still are

When we asked the logistics lead of a 3,000-hacker event what changes between organizing for 30 people and 3,000, the answer was: the fundamentals don't. Cultivate community. Encourage building and collaboration. Get people trying new technologies. Only the food order, the venue, and the Wi-Fi change.

My own version, from the participant side: in 48 hours you might not build a lot — but what changes isn't what you build, it's your mindset. The best organizers use AI to buy back operational time, then spend that time on the thing AI can't do: being present with their hackers.

## How to read this guide

The chapters follow the organizer's journey in roughly chronological order — purpose, timeline, team, participants, marketing, funding, logistics, experience, and follow-through. Each chapter combines:

1. **Patterns from interviews** — what 16 (and counting) real organizing teams actually do. Stories are anonymized; case studies are org-attributed with consent.
2. **Where AI helps** — specific, non-surface-level applications organizers are using today.
3. **Checklists** — the concrete artifacts you should have at each stage.
