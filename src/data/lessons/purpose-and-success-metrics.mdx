# Purpose and Success Metrics

Decide what your hackathon is for before you decide anything else. Scale changes the event more than you think.

## Purpose is a design constraint, not a mission statement

Every downstream decision — venue size, application process, sponsor mix, judging format — is really a purpose decision in disguise. Teams that skip this step end up with an event optimized for nothing in particular.

Common purposes, each with different design implications:

- **Learning-first** — optimize for first-time hackers, workshops, and mentorship density. Winners matter less than completion rates.
- **Community-first** — optimize for return attendance, unstructured social time, and organizer-hacker interaction.
- **Recruiting or sponsor-first** — optimize for polished demos, sponsor booths, and career touchpoints.
- **Innovation or theme-first** — optimize for a specific domain (health, climate, AI) with expert judges and domain challenges.

Pick one primary purpose. You can serve the others, but when two goals conflict at 2 AM on event day, the primary purpose is the tiebreaker.

## The scale trade-off is real

The most striking finding from our interviews: **large events stop feeling like hackathons.**

Organizers at Hackathon at Berkeley, who run both a 3,000+ hacker flagship in San Francisco and a smaller summer AI hackathon on campus, told us the flagship starts feeling "more like an expo" — while the smaller event keeps a calmer, more collaborative energy that they associate with the actual hacker spirit.

Scale isn't free. It costs:

- **Organizer attention.** More hackers require more organizers and volunteers per function, and coordination overhead grows faster than headcount.
- **Intimacy.** At 300 people, organizers know hackers' names. At 3,000, they manage crowd flow.
- **Purpose drift.** Sponsor obligations and production logistics start dictating the schedule.

None of this means "don't scale." It means scale is a purpose decision: an expo-style flagship is a legitimate event — if that's what you chose on purpose.

## Protect "dilly-dally time"

One of our favorite pieces of advice from the interviews: deliberately preserve unstructured time for organizers to wander the venue and talk to hackers. The Berkeley team calls this staying grounded — when organizers spend the whole event heads-down on logistics, they lose touch with the reason the event exists.

If your run of show has every organizer occupied every hour, your event is overplanned. Schedule slack.

## Metrics that match the purpose

Measure what your purpose says matters, not what's easy to count:

| Purpose | Leading metrics |
| --- | --- |
| Learning | % first-time hackers, % teams that submit, workshop attendance |
| Community | Return-attendee rate, organizer-hacker interactions, post-event community activity |
| Recruiting | Sponsor-hacker conversations, resume-book opt-ins, offers traced to the event |
| Innovation | Projects continued after the event, domain-expert judge scores |

Two reference points from our interviews: Hackathon at Berkeley's flagship runs at roughly **25–33% first-time hackers**; PASS Hackathon 2026 at UC Santa Cruz ran at **56% first-timers**. Neither number is "right" — they reflect different purposes. Know yours before the event, so the number you see afterward means something.

## Where AI helps

- **Survey synthesis.** Post-event feedback at any real scale is unreadable by hand. LLMs are genuinely good at clustering hundreds of free-text responses into themes with representative quotes — turning your feedback form into an actual instrument.
- **Application analytics.** The same tooling that helps review applications (see [Recruiting Participants](/recruiting-participants)) can profile your applicant pool — experience levels, school mix, interests — before you finalize programming.
- **Goal tracking.** A simple dashboard fed from your registration and submission systems answers "are we on track?" without a weekly manual count.

## Checklist

- One-sentence primary purpose, agreed by the whole leadership team
- Target scale chosen deliberately, with the expo trade-off discussed
- 3–5 metrics defined *before* the event, each traceable to the purpose
- Unstructured organizer time protected in the run of show
- Post-event survey drafted early, so day-of data collection isn't an afterthought
