# Purpose and Success Metrics

Decide what your hackathon is for before you decide anything else. Scale changes the event more than you think.

## Purpose is a design constraint, not a mission statement

Every downstream decision — venue size, application process, sponsor mix, judging format — is really a purpose decision in disguise. Teams that skip this step end up with an event optimized for nothing in particular.

Common purposes, each with different design implications:

- **Learning-first** — optimize for first-time hackers, mentorship density, and completion rates. One event we studied ran at 56% first-timers and accepted anyone with a serious application.
- **Community-first** — optimize for return attendance and unstructured social time. A six-season event in India deliberately holds a 70/30 new-to-returning ratio and runs a 7,000-member community year-round; the hackathon is just its annual peak.
- **Recruiting or sponsor-first** — optimize for polished demos and career touchpoints. One European org explicitly sells sponsors on watching how candidates "work, communicate, and solve problems" before hiring them.
- **Innovation or theme-first** — optimize for a domain (defense tech, quantum, healthcare) with expert judges and partner-set challenges.

Pick one primary purpose. When two goals conflict at 2 AM on event day, the primary purpose is the tiebreaker.

My own bias, for what it's worth: hackathons are the cradle of grassroots entrepreneurship, and the thing they actually produce is a mindset shift. In 48 hours most people won't build much — but standing close to founders and builders they admire, shipping something with strangers, changes what they believe they can do. Design for that, and the demos take care of themselves.

## The scale trade-off is real

The most striking finding across interviews: **large events stop feeling like hackathons.**

The team behind a 3,000+ hacker West Coast flagship told us candidly that past that scale the event "starts feeling less like a hackathon and more like an expo" — and that the single biggest improvement available to them might be scaling *down*. Their smaller summer event keeps the calm, collaborative energy they associate with the actual hacker spirit. Worse, their organizing team had *shrunk* year-over-year while the event grew — Wi-Fi failed at scale, and the unstructured time organizers use to stay connected to hackers evaporated.

Scale costs:

- **Organizer attention.** Coordination overhead grows faster than headcount, and the org must scale alongside the event — not after it.
- **Intimacy.** At 300 people, organizers know hackers' names. At 3,000, they manage crowd flow.
- **Purpose drift.** Sponsor obligations and production logistics start dictating the schedule.

None of this means "don't scale." It means scale is a purpose decision — an expo-style flagship is a legitimate event, *if that's what you chose on purpose*.

## Protect "dilly-dally time"

One of our favorite tactics from the interviews: that same flagship team deliberately schedules unstructured blocks for organizers to wander the floor and look at hackers' projects. They call it "dilly-dally time," and they treat losing it as a real cost — it's the grounding mechanism that reminds a team why they sit through sponsor calls and food-vendor negotiations. If your run of show has every organizer occupied every hour, your event is overplanned.

## Know your yield math

Purpose determines who you accept; yield math determines how many. The numbers from the field:

- A 1,000-hacker event in Southern California sees **~70% yield at acceptance→RSVP and ~70% again at RSVP→attendance** — stable across five years. They over-accept accordingly.
- The same event once "accepted the wrong number of people" and badly under-filled the venue — the two causes were a rival event the same weekend, and teammates being rejected while their friends got in (so entire teams no-showed). Their fix is in [Recruiting and Admissions](/recruiting-participants).
- A high-school organizer running a niche event saw **60 signups become 7 attendees** — free events for audiences with weak switching costs can see 90% no-show. RSVPs are not people.
- Meanwhile a 1,200-hacker Texas event has hackers **camping overnight in tents** for waitlist spots. Demand varies wildly; measure yours, don't assume.

## Metrics that match the purpose

| Purpose | Leading metrics |
| --- | --- |
| Learning | % first-time hackers, % teams that submit, feedback delivered per team |
| Community | Return-attendee rate, year-round community activity, organizer-hacker interactions |
| Recruiting | Sponsor-hacker conversations, resume-book opt-ins, offers traced to the event |
| Innovation | Projects continued after the event, partner-challenge submission counts |

Reference points from the field: 25–33% first-timers at one flagship, 56% at a 400-hacker campus event, a deliberate 70% at the community-first Indian event, ~50/50 at two others. None of these is "right" — they reflect different purposes. Know your number before the event so the number after means something.

One more metric, from an executive director's post-event realization: **sponsor retention tracks project submissions**, not workshop volume. Sponsors renew when hackers actually built on their stuff. If sponsors matter to you, "% of teams submitting to partner challenges" belongs on your dashboard.

## Where AI helps

- **Survey synthesis.** Post-event feedback at any real scale is unreadable by hand. LLMs cluster hundreds of free-text responses into themes with representative examples — turning the feedback form into an instrument.
- **Applicant-pool profiling.** The same tooling that supports review can profile your pool — experience levels, school mix, interests — before you finalize programming.
- **A live dashboard** fed from registration and submissions answers "are we on track?" without a weekly manual count.

## Checklist

- One-sentence primary purpose, agreed by the whole leadership team
- Target scale chosen deliberately, with the expo trade-off discussed
- Yield model written down (acceptance → RSVP → attendance) with over-acceptance buffer
- 3–5 metrics defined *before* the event, each traceable to the purpose
- Unstructured organizer time protected in the run of show
- Post-event survey drafted early, so day-of data collection isn't an afterthought
