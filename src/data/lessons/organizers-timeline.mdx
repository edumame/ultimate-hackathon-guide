# The Organizer's Timeline

A T-minus countdown from six months out to the closing ceremony, built from how real teams actually schedule the work.

## The shape every team converges on

Across sixteen interviews, the annual cycles rhyme remarkably: leadership locked in early, a long quiet build, an intense final three months, and a run-of-show lock one to three weeks out. Three real cycles, side by side:

- **A Stanford-scale flagship (winter event):** spring — select new leadership and transition; summer — rebuild the website and brand, align directors on direction, begin sponsor outreach, build internal tools; fall weeks 3–4 — recruit organizers, close hacker applications, set weekly application-reading quotas; winter — "chaos and surprise management"; final week — six-plus admin walkthroughs, directors working 8–9 hour days in a locked room.
- **A Florida event that doubled to 1,000 attendees (October event):** Feb–April — theme and lore, first sponsor prospectuses; May–July — *design everything* (merch, banners, website, palettes) by end of July, because student interns are back and busy in fall; Aug–Oct — in-person venue walkthroughs to "theatricize" the space and run-of-show work, with extra weight on opening and closing ceremonies.
- **A six-season event in India (1,500 hackers):** a 6-month process — sponsor one-on-ones from Sept–Oct, applications open 3 months out, and the final 3 months dense with pre-hackathon workshops that double as marketing.

The [MLH organizer guide's timeline](https://guide.mlh.io/general-information/hackathon-timeline) follows the same arc over a longer runway. Below, the countdown with what the field says about each phase.

## T-minus 6 to 4 months — Foundations

- Lock the **date and purpose**. Check the academic calendar, holidays, and — crucially — competing hackathons: one event we studied traced a serious attendance miss partly to a rival hackathon on the same campus the same weekend.
- **Finalize the leadership team.** Teams we interviewed had organizers confirmed by September for winter events. Late organizer recruiting is the root cause of most downstream slips.
- **Lock the venue.** Longest-lead item; everything depends on it.
- Put up a **placeholder website** with the date and an email signup.

## T-minus 4 to 3 months — Money and design

- Build the **budget** and **sponsorship prospectus**; start outreach now and set up the tracking system on day one (see [Sponsors and Funding](/sponsors-and-funding)).
- **Front-load design.** The Florida team's rule is worth stealing: every visual deliverable gets a first iteration before fall — you'll iterate as sponsors join, but nothing starts from zero during crunch.
- Open **organizer applications** if growing the team. Two separate teams use a small October "hack day" as a working tryout for both new organizers and their own systems.

## T-minus 3 to 1 months — The build

- **Confirm venue details** — rooms, power, wifi capacity at full load, food vendors.
- **Open hacker applications** and run promotion continuously (see [Marketing and Storytelling](/marketing-and-storytelling)).
- **Build the portals** — registration, check-in, submissions — as a steady workstream, not a final sprint. Every experienced team we interviewed builds or maintains internal tooling in this window.
- **Book judges, mentors, speakers**; plan workshops with sponsor partners. One large event targets 200+ judges and starts recruiting months out (see [why](/workshops-mentors-judges)).
- Track everything with **weekly full-team meetings** plus direct owner follow-ups — the cadence nearly every team independently converged on (Sundays and Mondays are popular).

## T-minus 2 weeks — Lock it

- **Finalize the run of show.** Most teams lock it 1–3 weeks out; one European org drafts it in April–May and finalizes one week before. Late enough to absorb changes, early enough to rehearse.
- Final headcount → food orders, badge printing, swag counts. **Physical goods need hard buffers**: one logistics director had badges stuck in customs and merch arriving the day before doors — she was fully prepared to drive six hours to retrieve them.
- **Reminder blasts** to participants; judge and mentor briefings; confirm rubrics.
- **Write the emergency plan** — and make your buffers bidirectional. One team had backup plans for late vendors; the vendors showed up 90 minutes *early*, colliding with the sponsor fair. Plan both directions.

## Game day

- Check-in tested before doors open — with a manual fallback. One president watched his brand-new digital check-in system fail on its first live run and solved it with a mid-event drive to Walmart for wristbands. "Be flexible like rubber."
- Meal distribution with buffer stock — surpluses are cheap insurance, shortages are memorable.
- Judging per plan, fallback ready ([the seizure story](/venue-logistics-run-of-show) is why).
- Protect organizer dilly-dally time — someone should always be free to just talk to hackers.

## After the event

The most neglected phase — it gets its own chapter: [Post-Event Follow-Up](/post-event-follow-up). And note when next year starts: the strongest organizers we've met start the next edition's plan *the morning after* — committee shape by the following month, sponsors through the fall.

## Where AI helps

- **Project management copilots.** Feed the timeline into a tracker and let an agent flag slipping dependencies ("venue contract unsigned at T-3 months blocks the headcount decision").
- **Reminder drafting.** Every T-minus milestone has an email attached. Draft the entire sequence in one sitting, then schedule it.
- **Institutional memory.** An AI assistant over past channels, docs, and decision logs is most valuable during the build, when every week produces a decision someone will re-ask about next year — especially for teams whose entire leadership rotates annually.

## Checklist

- Date, venue, and leadership team locked ≥4 months out
- Rival-event calendar checked before locking the date
- Sponsorship prospectus and outreach tracker live ≥3 months out
- All design deliverables drafted before the final quarter
- Weekly full-team meeting cadence running through the build
- Run of show locked at T-1 to T-3 weeks, with a full run-through
- Emergency plan written — buffers in both directions — before doors open
