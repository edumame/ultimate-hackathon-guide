# Recruiting Participants and Admissions

Application review at scale, team-linked admissions, the yield math nobody teaches you, and designing for first-time hackers.

## The demand spectrum is wider than you think

Before tactics, calibrate: a 1,200-hacker flagship pulled 3,000–3,500 applications with almost no marketing — a website, word of mouth, and two Instagram posts — and has waitlisted hackers **camping overnight in tents** for no-show spots. An elite-university flagship gets 15,000 applications for 1,000 seats. one of China's largest student-run hackathons: 6,928 applications, ~10% acceptance. Meanwhile a high-school organizer's carefully planned event for underrepresented students got 60 signups and **7 attendees**, and a first-ever college hackathon fought for every registration. Your strategy depends entirely on which end of this spectrum you're on — brand compounds, and until you have one, distribution is the whole game.

## Channels that actually filled rooms

- **The school email blast.** Multiple teams got their engineering school to email every student — one official email outperforms weeks of posters. The administrative relationship is the real work.
- **Professors as distribution.** One Canadian organizer's team uses AI to scrape professor contact databases across universities, then emails professors asking them to post the event to their class pages — routing through instructors who already have the class's attention.
- **Cross-promotion with other hackathons**, ambassador programs, and hacker-exchange deals (several flagships auto-accept a handful of each other's organizers — cheap goodwill that spreads your name).
- **What failed:** one organizer emailed every school district in and around a major city — public and private, with flyers — and got *zero* attendees from any of them. Institutional email to minors' gatekeepers is a dead channel; go where the students actually are.

## Admissions: the four working models

1. **Hand-read everything, twice.** One flagship has every application read by ≥2 directors across three rounds (priority, regular, late) spanning a month, ~5–6 hours per director for the priority round alone. Their selection bar is character, not skill: would this person, at 2 AM on their third Red Bull with their own code breaking, help the stranger next to them? They reframe review as a perk — a rare chance to sit with hackers' passions before the event.
2. **Rubric + leaderboard at volume.** Another flagship scores every application on three axes — technical ability, creativity, passion — where passion is operationalized as *likelihood of showing up* (naming a specific track is a strong signal). Every app is scored at least twice by a 40-person review corps, with a **grader leaderboard** (prizes for the top three; one grader read 844 applications) and per-grader statistics so no reviewer is systematically harsh or generous.
3. **Triple-review at mega-volume.** The Chinese org routes all ~7,000 applications through three independent internal reviewers on a self-built portal.
4. **Accept the serious.** The 400-hacker campus event reads all 500+ applications (split evenly across 12 organizers) and accepts anyone with a well-written, serious response — a light filter matched to a learning-first purpose.

Pick the model that matches your volume and purpose. All four teams agree on one thing: **someone human reads every application.**

## Team-linked admissions: the single best yield fix we found

One of these flagships traced a painful attendance miss to a structural cause: applicants applied solo, and when one teammate was rejected, the *whole team* silently no-showed. Their fix — applicants can link applications into teams of up to four; if **two or more members clear the bar, the entire team is accepted**. Whole groups get in, whole groups show up. Complete-team admissions attacks the no-show problem at its root: people attend with their friends or not at all.

Layer on the standard yield machinery: over-accept against your measured yield (~70% × ~70% at that event, stable for five years), RSVP deadlines with one-click extensions, waitlist backfill on-site, and — from the community-first Indian event — the full engagement press: a **team video submission with the application** (forcing teammates to meet before the event), a personal phone call to every accepted team, touchpoints every alternate day, buses from the airport, and prizes for the first 20 teams to arrive. Their line: guests should "feel like home" and have to plan nothing except attending.

## The AI-application question

Every team has now had to take a position:

- **Auto-reject:** one flagship team instantly rejects GPT-written essays — unannounced. The AI essay itself signals low effort, and effort is their best attendance predictor.
- **Tolerate:** the campus event accepts AI-assisted applications knowingly — "you still have to feed it real information," so a thoughtful response processed through AI still signals intent.
- **Refuse AI in review:** two flagships hand-read everything as a matter of identity ("absolutely not").
- **Reject AI review for fairness:** one executive director considered AI screening and rejected it — black-box decisions with no human input, unfair to applicants with heterogeneous materials, and expensive at 5,500 applications. She remains open to it; 11,000 human reviews "is a lot."

Whatever your policy, the durable answer is better questions: ask for specifics (what did you build, what broke, link the coolest thing you've made) that are cheap for genuine builders and expensive to fake.

## Design for first-timers deliberately

First-timer share ranged from 25–33% at one flagship to 56% at the campus event — and the community event *engineers* ≥70% new hackers on purpose. If half your room has never hacked, that's not a side audience; it's the audience. It should shape team formation (one flagship runs a Slack channel + a matching algorithm that deliberately seeds experienced hackers with beginners + an in-person mixer — "nobody who flew across the country should end up teamless"), workshop levels, mentor density, and how judging feedback is delivered. My own research says the deeper reason this matters: novices have the most *variable* experience at hackathons, and that variance — not the average — is what decides whether they ever come back.

## Where AI helps

- **Surface, don't decide.** The pattern with the best risk/reward: AI highlights key details in each application so human reviewers spend attention on judgment instead of scanning. (Adoption is genuinely mixed — some flagship teams refuse even this.)
- **Reviewer consistency.** Flag applications where two readers diverged sharply so a third adjudicates the borderline cases rather than random ones.
- **Professor/channel research** at scale, per the professor-scraping pipeline above.
- **Comms sequences** — acceptance, RSVP nudge, logistics, day-before hype — drafted in one sitting, segmented first-timer vs. veteran.

## Checklist

- School/administrative email channel secured; dead channels (district blasts) skipped
- Review model chosen to match volume; every application touched by a human
- Team-linked admissions or an equivalent whole-group acceptance rule
- Yield model with over-acceptance buffer; RSVP + reminder sequence scheduled
- AI-application policy decided (and reflected in what your questions ask for)
- First-timer percentage tracked and fed into team formation, workshops, and mentoring
