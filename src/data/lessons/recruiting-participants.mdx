# Recruiting Participants

Promotion channels, application review at scale, and designing for first-time hackers.

## Channels that actually filled rooms

From the interviews, the channels that produced real registrations:

- **The engineering school email blast.** Both teams we interviewed got their school to email every engineering student. One official email outperforms weeks of posters. Building the administrative relationship is the real work.
- **Legacy and brand.** Established events lean on years of accumulated reputation in the collegiate hackathon community. If you don't have legacy, borrow it: partner hackathons cross-promoting each other was a core tactic for the Santa Cruz team.
- **Regular social cadence.** Not one announcement — a steady drumbeat, with posts spiking around application deadlines.
- **Community-specific outreach.** Reaching out directly to underrepresented tech groups both diversifies the room and taps networks your general promotion misses.

## Applications: three rounds and hand review

The strongest application process we've documented, from Hackathon at Berkeley:

- **Three application rounds** — priority, regular, and late — collectively spanning about a month. Rounds create urgency (apply early for better odds) and smooth the review load.
- **Every application is hand-read by at least two directors.** For priority-round volume, directors each spend roughly 5–6 hours reviewing.
- **The selection criterion is collaborative builders** — their framing: would this person help someone else at 2 AM when the code is breaking? Not raw skill. Not resume.

The Santa Cruz team, with 500+ applications and 12 organizers, split the pile evenly across the whole team and accepted anyone with well-written, serious responses — a lighter-touch filter matched to their capacity and learning-first purpose.

Use an **RSVP confirmation system** after acceptance, and send reminders as the event approaches — acceptance-to-attendance drop-off is where headcounts die.

## Design for first-timers deliberately

First-time hackers were 25–33% of Berkeley's flagship and **56%** at Santa Cruz. If half your room has never hacked before, that's not a side audience — that's your audience. It should shape team formation events, workshop levels, mentor density, and how judging feedback is delivered.

Ask on the application. Know the number before the event, and design for it.

## Where AI helps

This is the area with the clearest AI adoption among top-tier events:

- **Application surfacing, not application deciding.** TreeHacks uses AI to bold and surface key details in applications so human reviewers focus attention where it matters. This is the pattern to copy: AI compresses reading time, humans keep judgment. The "would they help at 2 AM" call stays human.
- **Two-reader consistency.** With multiple reviewers, an LLM pass can flag applications where readers diverged sharply, so a third reader adjudicates the borderline cases rather than random ones.
- **Applicant-pool profiling.** Cluster the pool by experience and interests before finalizing workshops and challenge tracks.
- **Comms sequences.** Acceptance, RSVP nudge, logistics, day-before hype — draft the full sequence in one sitting, personalized by applicant segment (first-timer vs. veteran).

A caution from the field: reviewers told us AI-written applications are increasingly common. The answer isn't an arms race of detectors — it's application questions that ask for specifics (what did you build, what broke, what would you build here) that are cheap for a genuine builder to answer and expensive to fake convincingly.

## Checklist

- School/administrative email channel secured
- Promotion calendar with deadline-spike cadence
- Application rounds scheduled with review-hours budgeted per reader
- Selection criterion written down and shared with every reviewer
- First-timer percentage tracked and fed into program design
- RSVP + reminder sequence scheduled through event day
