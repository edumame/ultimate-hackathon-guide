# Marketing and Storytelling

Annual marketing arcs, serialized themes, viral moments, and why one event doubled applications with a real campaign.

## Marketing is a year, not a month

The most sophisticated marketing operation in our interviews belongs to a 1,500-person European hackathon that runs the classic **AIDA funnel as an annual calendar**:

- **Awareness (Q1):** make the logo ubiquitous — heavy Instagram content, sponsoring student parties, LinkedIn. Explicitly *not* converting anyone yet; just exposure.
- **Interest (spring):** run smaller themed hackathons (defense tech, quantum, a midnight-sun event above the Arctic Circle) and film everything, so people see what the org actually does.
- **Desire (summer):** the quiet period — outreach through builder communities and their channels.
- **Action (September):** when school restarts, physical word-of-mouth at student events, deliberately manufacturing FOMO for the November flagship.

Each smaller event runs its own compressed funnel — "smaller triangles building the big triangle." And their marketing lead is refreshingly honest that the framework is scaffolding, not science: a lot of marketing "is based on feeling," and virality can't be scheduled.

## Storyline marketing

A 1,200-hacker Texas event themes each year like a franchise — last year "Lost in the Pages," a storybook fantasy, with a produced storyline video. Their self-critique: the video only premiered at the opening ceremony, where it could no longer sell a single ticket. This year's plan is the fix worth copying: **release the story episodically in the weeks before the event, building to a finale episode at opening ceremony.** Theme chosen in spring, storyboarding over summer, content ready before fall.

Why bother? Their marketing lead's framing: hackers shouldn't come "just because it's a coding event" — the product is the whole experience, and the story is the wrapper that makes a hackathon feel like a world instead of a schedule. A Florida president says the same thing with a different word: he aims for a "Disney experience" — full immersion and theming, so people feel nostalgic and come back.

The proof this matters: that Texas event pulled 3,500 applications with *almost no marketing* — a website and two Instagram posts. Their thesis for this year is that a real campaign, started at the end of summer, doubles it. Latent demand is real; storytelling is how you collect it.

## Channels: ruthlessness beats presence

- **Instagram + LinkedIn only** is a defensible strategy. The Texas team tested TikTok — it flopped despite the student demographic — and deliberately skips YouTube (long-form retention isn't worth the production cost).
- **One viral moment can transform demand.** A Southern California event's Instagram video hit ~700K views and applications rose 83% year-over-year. You can't schedule virality, but you can produce enough swings at it.
- **Classroom announcements still work.** The same event requires every organizer to sign up for two lectures and give a five-minute pitch. Physical, unglamorous, effective.
- **Design quality is marketing.** The Florida president is blunt: detail-oriented students judge events by their posters and website — "if I don't like the look of something, I won't go." His team finishes first iterations of *every* visual deliverable by end of July.
- **Content creators, paid and free-range.** The Texas team is building a program to fly out 10–15 tech-adjacent creators, pay them, and let them choose their mode — film, judge, mentor, or just enjoy. Authentic enjoyment converts better than sponsored promotion; creator relationships also route into sponsorships via their employers.

## Shoot the event like it's next year's campaign

The European org films with intent: an after-movie for storytelling, per-partner challenge videos for the opening ceremony, and — the clever part — **partner-testimonial interviews shot during the event** ("how would you describe the event?", "most interesting project you saw?") specifically as sales collateral for next year's sponsor pipeline.

And back up the footage. The Texas team lost *every recording* from last year's event to a storage failure — a year of marketing raw material, gone.

## Two cautionary tales

- **The channel that reaches gatekeepers reaches no one.** A high-school organizer emailed every school district around a major city and got zero attendees from any of them. Marketing to institutions is not marketing to people.
- **Word-of-mouth has a ceiling — but it's high.** The European org recruits its entire paid team by word-of-mouth with zero open applications, and several events fill on legacy alone. If your event is young, you don't have this asset yet; you're building it. Every event is partly a marketing campaign for the next one.

## Where AI helps

Marketing is where organizers volunteered the heaviest AI adoption:

- **Content ideation and iteration.** The Texas marketing lead uses AI "for literally everything" — ideation especially. Iteration speed on promotional content is the visible win at multiple orgs.
- **Personalized outreach hooks.** The European partnerships head has AI dig up company-specific angles — it found a prospect's 100th anniversary and built the invite around their centenary.
- **Serialized content production.** Episodic storyline campaigns are storyboard-heavy; AI collapses the cost of scripts, shot lists, and variant cuts.
- **Design assets.** Experiments with brand-consistent AI-generated visuals are early but promising; the same org that hand-makes everything concedes "massive potential" for posters.

## Checklist

- Annual marketing arc sketched (even a rough AIDA calendar), not just a launch post
- Theme and storyline chosen in spring; content produced before fall
- Channel strategy decided by evidence, not presence-by-default
- Every organizer pitching in classrooms or equivalent physical channels
- Event filmed with next year's sponsor pipeline and campaign in mind — with backups
- Design deliverables treated as marketing, finished early, held to a taste bar
