# Workshops, Mentors, and Judges

Partnering with speakers on programming, aligning judges on a rubric, and where AI judging tools fit.

## Workshops: co-design with sponsors and speakers

Neither team we interviewed programs workshops top-down. They're decided **in partnership with the speakers and sponsor companies** — the sponsor brings the content and the staff, the organizers bring the audience and the slot. This is also a big part of what sponsors are buying (see [Sponsors and Funding](/sponsors-and-funding)).

What organizers should still own:

- **The level mix.** If half your room is first-timers, half your workshops should assume zero setup. Match the mix to your applicant data.
- **The schedule position.** Intro workshops belong in the first hours, when teams are forming and hacking hasn't started. API-specific sponsor workshops belong after teams have committed to ideas.
- **Beyond-the-laptop programming.** Memorable events program non-technical texture — the Santa Cruz team's logistics director organized a weather balloon launch as a shared spectacle. Mini-events, midnight snacks, and rituals do community work that workshops can't.

## Mentors: density over prestige

Mentors are the highest-leverage people at a learning-first event — more than judges or speakers. Recruit from sponsor engineers, upper-year students, alumni, and local professionals. Match mentor coverage to your first-timer percentage, brief them to *unblock rather than build*, and make them findable: a help queue, a mentor table, colored lanyards — anything beats "wander and hope."

## Judging: rubric alignment is the hard part

The pattern from the field on judging mechanics:

- **~3 judges per team**, with 100+ teams at the Santa Cruz event — judge recruitment is a numbers game, so start early.
- **A shared online rubric, scored via Google Forms, with aggregated results.** Unglamorous and completely functional at real scale.
- **Alignment before scoring.** The rubric only works if judges interpret it the same way. Brief judges together, walk one example project through the rubric, and state explicitly what matters (learning? polish? technical depth?) — it follows from your event's purpose.

Design a judging flow that survives disruption: the Santa Cruz team's shift to fully-virtual judging during a medical emergency worked *because* their scoring already lived in a form, not on paper ([see the full story](/venue-logistics-run-of-show)).

Two structural choices to make deliberately:

1. **Demo format** — science-fair floor demos scale better and feel more energetic; stage pitches are higher-stakes and slower. Most large events use floor demos with a finalist stage round.
2. **Feedback path** — first-timers benefit enormously from written judge feedback. If your purpose is learning, build it into the form.

## Where AI helps

Judging is where dedicated AI tooling for organizers is emerging fastest — tools like **Harp** and **Jury** target exactly this workflow:

- **Score aggregation and normalization.** Correcting for harsh vs. generous judges across hundreds of scores is a real statistical problem that spreadsheets handle badly.
- **Assignment logistics.** Matching judges to teams under constraints (expertise, conflicts of interest, room geography, time) is a scheduling problem AI tools solve in seconds.
- **Submission triage.** At 100+ submissions, AI summaries of each project (stack, what it does, what's demo-able) help judges arrive at each table oriented instead of cold.
- **Feedback synthesis.** Turning three judges' rough notes into one coherent, kind feedback paragraph per team makes written feedback feasible at scale.

The line to hold: **AI assists judging operations; humans judge.** Ranking creative work is the judgment call your judges are there to make — and hackers can tell when it's outsourced.

## Checklist

- Workshop slate co-designed with sponsors, leveled to your applicant mix
- At least one non-technical spectacle or ritual programmed
- Mentor coverage matched to first-timer percentage, with a findability system
- Judges recruited early against a ~3-per-team target
- Rubric + digital scoring form tested; judge alignment briefing scheduled
- Judging fallback path defined (virtual/async)
