# Team Structure and Roles

How real organizing teams are structured — from 27 students running a 1,000-hacker flagship to a paid six-person company with 300 volunteers.

## Five real structures

The range across our interviews is wider than any single template:

- **The lean flagship: ~27–30 people for 1,000+ hackers.** One elite-university event runs on 6 co-directors plus ~21–27 organizers, coordinating mostly over plain text messages. A comparable 1,000-hacker event runs 44 organizers across 6 teams — and **zero volunteers**, having concluded that getting volunteers "on the same page" costs more than it returns.
- **The 12-person crew.** A 400-attendee campus event ran on 12 core organizers plus 4 volunteers — with a board including people who founded the event ten years earlier, carrying institutional memory across generations.
- **The democratic org: 26 people, three tiers.** A West Coast flagship structures as new members → directors (frontline execution) → leads → exec, across 8 sub-teams, with decisions made democratically and a mentorship program pairing every new member with a veteran from a different team.
- **The company: 6–10 paid staff + 200–300 volunteers.** One of Europe's largest hackathons is an actual company under a startup foundation — full-time student employees (below-market pay, one-year role tenure), sales volunteers on 5–10% commission, and volunteer job descriptions with explicit cadence expectations ("website lead: one blog post weekly").
- **The remote org: 45 people, fully distributed.** One of China's largest student-run hackathons runs 45 members across four departments entirely remotely, with a 10-hour-per-week minimum commitment screened at recruitment, 15–30 minute weekly team digests, and no all-hands until 1–2 months before the event.

The anti-patterns are just as instructive: a high-school organizer who has run teams of 7, 10, 15, and 21 told us the 21-person team simply "didn't work" — accountability dissolved. Her sweet spot: 7–10 with clear per-person roles.

## Recruit for initiative — and actually interview

The single most painful first-timer lesson in our interviews came from a first-ever college hackathon: the lead skipped interviews and "just gathered around people" who seemed interested. Twenty joined; many went quiet; and because they were friends and classmates, the accountability conversations were agonizing. The twist: most of the inactive members *were* genuinely interested — they just never had capacity. Enthusiasm at signup is not capacity. Screen for capacity.

What experienced teams do instead:

- **Recruit against skill gaps, not headcount targets.** No arbitrary team-size goals; map what the team lacks and hire that.
- **Use a working tryout.** Two teams independently use a small October event as a live audition for prospective organizers — it filters for the one trait you can't interview for: follow-through. A no-show without a serious reason answers the question.
- **Let candidates pitch a build.** One flagship's culture is a "build lead" system: anyone can pitch a project (a custom PCB badge, a photo booth, an AI-argument vending machine), and whoever pitches it owns it end-to-end. A freshman got hired partly by arriving at the interview with a build idea — and then solo-shipped 1,000 custom badges, funded by a sponsorship he closed himself.
- **Hire personality for people-facing roles.** The head of partnerships at the European org picks people with *any* real sales or customer-service experience — even selling shoes by phone — over more technically impressive candidates. An executive director told us the same: hard skills are teachable; hire organizers for being personable and communicative.
- **Ask for a multi-semester commitment.** One flagship requires ≥3 semesters; organizing knowledge compounds, and continuity is how.

## Culture, retention, and the corporate line

Most teams run on no formal incentive structure at all — "we don't dangle a teddy bear," as one lead put it. The teams describe themselves as friends who happen to organize a hackathon. But the strongest teams invest deliberately in what keeps volunteers around:

- **Real perks.** One large event gives organizers priority internship routing at sponsor companies, premium merch, an annual December retreat in another city, conference tickets, and reciprocal auto-acceptance at peer hackathons. Another event runs three retreats a year.
- **Respecting time.** One president admitted he'd habitually messaged teammates at 11 PM–1 AM as treasurer; nobody complained, and he now believes it quietly bred resentment. His new norm: "we value your work, so we won't message you at 1 AM." When he introduced structure — written expectations, a re-application that's really an expectations conversation — some members read it as the club "going corporate." Adding accountability without killing the friend-group vibe is a real tension; name it openly.
- **The honesty norm.** After a three-year veteran organizer vanished mid-cycle (it turned out he was struggling and needed a break), one team adopted a simple rule: if you can't do something, just tell us so we can account for it. Honor system — but silent non-performers aren't invited back.

## Bus factors and succession

The scariest story in our interviews: a logistics director got the flu one week before her event and realized her job *could not be handed off* — the entire logistics state lived in her head and one spreadsheet. Explaining it would have taken longer than doing it. "If anything happened to me the week before the event, things would just fall apart."

Continuity mechanisms from the field:

- **Documented playbooks.** The European org's whole leadership rotates every 1–2 years; playbooks plus a board of advisors (alumni founders who return to give back) carry the institution. one of China's largest student-run hackathons compiled its execution playbook and now shares it with peer orgs.
- **Boards and alumni.** Ten-year-old events keep founding members on a board; one flagship's founder returns every year; another org's alumni fly in from across the country for a post-event dinner.
- **Succession by referral + shadowing.** The European org's new CTO was recruited by the outgoing CTO and volunteered at one event first. Lightweight, but it works — barely. Their platform still has a bus factor of one developer.
- **Programs die with their champions.** One flagship ran a fellowship turning winners into founders; it faded when its champion graduated and funding lapsed. Programs need succession plans, not just founders.

## Where AI helps

- **Onboarding compression.** An AI assistant over past docs, decision logs, and chat history shortens the multi-semester ramp — the highest-leverage internal AI adoption we've seen at a top-tier org.
- **Meeting leverage.** Auto-transcribed weeklies with action-item extraction make the sync meeting produce an artifact.
- **The bus-factor antidote.** That logistics director's wish list was specific: an AI agent drafting her admin emails and a dashboard tracking all nine of her admin threads. If a role's state lives in one head, AI-assisted documentation is the cheapest insurance available.

## Checklist

- Functions mapped to named owners before the build starts
- Actual interviews (or a working tryout) for every organizer — including friends
- Commitment expectations (hours/week, semesters) stated at recruitment
- Weekly all-hands with owned action items; personal follow-ups between meetings
- Every critical role documented well enough to survive a week of flu
- A continuity mechanism: playbook, board, alumni advisors, or all three
