# How a 12-person team runs a 400-hacker event

> **Draft case study.** Based on an organizer interview from February 2026. The organization is anonymized until its team approves publication; details are drawn from the interview transcript.

## Snapshot

This decade-old campus hackathon ran a three-day winter event with **over 400 attendees** and six speakers, organized by just **12 core members** plus 4 volunteers, mentors, and judges. They received **500+ applications**, raised **~$33.5K** from a barbell mix of national brands and startups, and ran a room that was **56% first-time hackers**, mostly local.

## What they do differently

**They use an event to recruit their organizers.** Rather than interviewing for initiative, they pilot a "hack day" mini-event in mid-fall and have prospective organizers help run it — a working tryout that filters for follow-through before anyone joins the core team. A no-show without a serious reason answers the question.

**They carry a decade of institutional memory.** The team structure is president → directors → organizers, with a **board that includes people who started the hackathon ten years ago** plus supporting faculty. Continuity isn't left to chance.

**They design for first-timers as the majority.** At 56% first-time hackers, the beginner experience is the main product — light-touch acceptance (any well-written, serious application gets in), heavy promotion through the engineering school, and beyond-the-laptop programming like a weather balloon launch coordinated by their logistics director.

## How they run it

- **Timeline:** organizers finalized by September, the hack-day tryout in October, then a **three-month build** — venue and resources confirmed first, sponsorships worked until the final weeks, portals built by the engineering team as a steady workstream. Run of show locked **two weeks out**.
- **Funding:** cold email via directories, LinkedIn outreach, and personal connections; a dedicated sponsor email plus a spreadsheet tracking every send and response; pitch sequence of interest email → call with stats and deck → sponsorship package. Their outreach rule: personalized, precise, and blunt — sponsors are busy people.
- **Communication:** **Discord for participants** (easy onboarding), **Slack for internal ops**, weekly Sunday meetings, and direct emails between directors and organizers.
- **Applications:** 500+ responses split evenly across all 12 organizers; everyone reads.
- **Judging:** ~3 judges per team across 100+ teams, an online rubric with aggregated scores.

## What surprised them

Mid-event, a medical emergency halted everything — including in-person judging. Because scoring already lived in an online form, they **shifted to fully virtual judging on the spot** and continued with only a 30–45 minute delay. Resilience came from the format choice, not from heroics.

Their biggest stated learning matches nearly every team we've interviewed: **clear communication.** Their version of the story was two directors independently pursuing the same marquee tech sponsor through different contacts — the fix being that everyone knows who owns which relationship, and developments are shared across departments immediately. Also instructive: of four volunteers who showed up the first evening, only one lasted the weekend — the 12 organizers absorbed it. Plan as if volunteers won't persist.

## The one thing to copy

**The hack-day organizer tryout.** One small fall event solves three problems at once: it tests prospective organizers on real work, gives the team a live rehearsal of their systems, and markets the main event — all months before game day.
