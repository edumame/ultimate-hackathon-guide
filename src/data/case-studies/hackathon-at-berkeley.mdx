# How Hackathon at Berkeley runs it

> **Draft case study.** Based on an organizer interview from February 2026. Attribution is organization-level; details are pending review with the organizing team before final publication.

## Snapshot

Hackathon at Berkeley (formerly CalHacks) runs **two events a year**: a flagship hackathon each fall in San Francisco with **over 3,000 hackers**, and a smaller AI hackathon on the UC Berkeley campus in summer. The organizing team is **26 people**, structured democratically, with **25–33% first-time hackers** at the flagship.

## What they do differently

**They run two very different events on purpose — and learn opposite lessons from each.** The flagship teaches them how to operate at expo scale; the summer AI hackathon preserves the calmer, more collaborative energy they associate with the actual hacker spirit. Organizers were candid that past ~3,000 hackers, the event "starts feeling less like a hackathon and more like an expo" — so they keep a smaller event in the portfolio deliberately.

**They're structured as a democracy, not a hierarchy.** Three tiers — **Cubs** (new members), **Directors** (larger-scale organizers), and an **Executive** tier that sets organizational goals — across eight sub-teams. There's no formal incentive or accountability structure: they recruit self-motivated builders and describe themselves as "a bunch of friends that just so happen to organize a hackathon together."

**They hand-read everything.** Every application is read by at least two directors, across three rounds (priority, regular, late) spanning about a month, with directors spending 5–6 hours each on the priority round. The selection bar is not skill — it's whether someone would help a stranger at 2 AM when the code is breaking.

## How they run it

- **Recruiting the team:** no arbitrary headcount targets — they recruit against specific skill gaps, reach out to underrepresented tech groups and past volunteers, and ask for a minimum three-semester commitment so context compounds.
- **Recruiting hackers:** legacy and brand recognition in the collegiate hackathon community does heavy lifting, plus an engineering-school email to every student.
- **Coordination:** weekly Sunday all-hands, personal email follow-ups between organizers, and **walkie-talkies** on the event floor.
- **Judging:** shared online rubric via Google Forms with aggregated scoring.

## What surprised them

The biggest lesson from their AI hackathon was not technical — it was **team communication across eight sub-teams**. Their sharpest story: two teams pursued NVIDIA as a sponsor concurrently, with overlapping outreach and no clarity on ownership or how a joint pitch would work. The fix they took away: everyone must know who is doing what, and new developments get broadcast to the whole team immediately.

Also notable: when asked whether they wanted better hackathon-management software, the answer was "nice to have, not a pain point." Their bottleneck is coordination and purpose, not tooling.

## The one thing to copy

**Protect "dilly-dally time."** They deliberately preserve unstructured time for organizers to wander and interact with hackers — staying grounded in the purpose of the event. Their advice to new organizers: the fundamentals are identical at 30 or 3,000 people (cultivate community, encourage building, get people trying new things), and when it gets stressful, slow down and remember it's for the hackers.
