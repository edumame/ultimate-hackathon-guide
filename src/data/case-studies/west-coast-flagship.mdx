# How a 3,000-hacker flagship runs it

> **Draft case study.** Based on an organizer interview from February 2026. The organization is anonymized until its team approves publication; details are drawn from the interview transcript.

## Snapshot

This decade-old West Coast organization runs **two events a year**: a flagship hackathon each fall with **over 3,000 hackers**, and a smaller AI hackathon on campus in summer — roughly half the size. The organizing team is **26 people** across eight sub-teams, structured democratically, with **25–33% first-time hackers** at the flagship.

## What they do differently

**They run two very different events on purpose — and learn opposite lessons from each.** The flagship teaches them how to operate at expo scale; the summer event preserves the calmer, more collaborative energy they associate with the actual hacker spirit. The organizers were candid that past ~3,000 hackers the event "starts feeling less like a hackathon and more like an expo" — and that the biggest improvement available to the flagship might be scaling it *down*, not up. One team runs both events, so summer learnings transfer directly into the fall.

**They're structured as a democracy, not a hierarchy.** Four tiers — new members, directors (frontline execution), leads (team direction and new initiatives), and an executive tier that sets org-wide goals — but "everyone has the same amount of influence." There's no formal incentive or accountability structure: they recruit self-motivated builders, pair every new member with veteran mentors from other teams, and describe themselves as a bunch of friends who happen to organize a hackathon together. Even first-semester members run real initiatives — one launched a volunteer training program, ordered engraved finalist gifts, and hosted a community-college outreach webinar in his first term.

**They hand-read everything.** Every application is read by at least two directors, across three rounds (priority, regular, late) spanning about a month — rejected priority applicants get a fresh pair of readers in the regular round — with directors spending 5–6 hours each on the priority round. The selection bar is not skill: it's whether someone, at 2 AM on their third energy drink with their own code breaking, would help the stranger next to them.

## How they run it

- **Recruiting the team:** no headcount targets — they recruit against specific skill gaps, source from underrepresented-in-tech clubs and past event volunteers, and ask for a minimum three-semester commitment so context compounds.
- **Recruiting hackers:** a decade of legacy in the collegiate hackathon community does heavy lifting — on one sponsor call, an engineer at a major cloud company lit up remembering he'd attended their second-ever edition as a hacker.
- **Coordination:** weekly Sunday all-hands, personal follow-ups between organizers, and **walkie-talkies** on the event floor — adopted after a single physical room key became the bottleneck for eight sub-teams.
- **Judging:** shared online rubric with aggregated scoring.

## What surprised them

Scale bites in ordinary places: Wi-Fi failed at 3,000 hackers, and the organizing team had actually *shrunk* from the previous year while the event grew. Even a finalist-gift plush bear turned out to be a four-department project — logistics, finance, sponsorship, and design all touching one stuffed animal.

They also lost most of their **"dilly-dally time"** — deliberately scheduled unstructured blocks for organizers to wander and talk to hackers — and count that as a real cost, because it's the mechanism that keeps the team grounded in why the event exists.

## The one thing to copy

**Protect dilly-dally time.** Their advice to new organizers: the fundamentals are identical at 30 or 3,000 people — cultivate community, encourage building, get people trying new things — and when it gets stressful, slow down and remember it's for the hackers.
